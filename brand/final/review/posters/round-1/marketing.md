# Posters (notice family), round 1: marketing notes

Reviewed 14 Oct 2026 against the A4 renders in this folder and the tenth-size thumbnails. Judged on message, hierarchy and behaviour, not letterforms (the renders are Inter; the final is Avenir Next). Read with the flyer round's `marketing.md`, whose frame still holds.

## The frame, again

The flyers were built on one fact: a landlord prints a sign they wanted anyway, not a campaign poster, and a drinker fourth in the line needs permission, an instruction and reassurance, in that order. The two reference photos add a second fact. **A pub wall already has a register for telling customers what to do, and it is the notice:** a flat field, a big instruction, a pictogram, a polite line, a small mark. "NO SMOKING OR VAPING" doesn't persuade anyone; it states the house's position. That is the register a landlord is comfortable putting up, because every other sign on the wall uses it. The flyer is the campaign's voice on the pub's wall. These are the pub's voice, borrowed from the campaign. Both are useful; they do different jobs.

Two more things the references teach:

- **One pictogram beats a paragraph.** The tap poster's whole argument is a tap. Ours should be the queue, crossed out, or the Cluster. The roundel already draws the right behaviour, so on the "do" poster the brand mark can be the picture.
- **The polite line is where the manners go.** "Please save water. Cheers." and "We kindly ask that you refrain from…" are the same move: the big type is blunt, the small type is kind. Our dry voice fits the small type well ("Please don’t queue. Cheers.").

What doesn't change: no figure that could be read as the pub's waiting time (none of the six has one), nothing for sale, the gender-neutral copy, and the jokes signed by the campaign rather than put in the pub's mouth.

## Who prints which

- **The prohibition notice (A, D)** is for the landlord who has had enough. It goes behind the bar facing the room, or on the pillar at the end of the bar where the line forms. "This bar does The Cluster." is their permission line; the polite ask is their manners. They will print it on the office printer, which means **the mono version is the one most pubs will actually hang**, and the colour one is for the pub that laminates things.
- **The instruction poster (B, E)** is for the pub that likes the campaign: the craft bar, the brewery taproom, the pub with a chalkboard sense of humour. It is cheerful, it has the roundel big, and it says what to do rather than what not to. It also works as the "do" half next to the notice's "don't".
- **The split (C)** is for nobody in particular: it tries to be both, and a landlord choosing one sheet will pick the one in their voice.

## Concept A, "No queueing: the prohibition notice" (red)

**Landlord.** A red sign with a crossed-out queue is a sign they understand, and "This bar does The Cluster." under it makes it theirs. But the headline is the campaign's name in the campaign's lowercase, and on the wall that reads as an advert for a website before it reads as a house rule. "NO QUEUEING AT THE BAR" (D's line) is the register they'd choose.

**Drinker.** From the door: the red, the sign, "don’t queue at the bar." The instruction is in the small type ("spread out along the bar"), which the tenth doesn't show. Red is a telling-off colour, which is fine for a notice (nobody is offended by "no smoking") but it is the one colour here that teases the customer rather than the behaviour. The house line softens it.

**Calls to action.** The foot is right: "Sign the petition." beside the mark, the address under it, the code at the right. One scan reason. Keep.

**Copy.** "We kindly ask that you spread out along the bar. The bar staff know who’s next." is the right polite line; it is the pub speaking, and it is the instruction and the reassurance in one breath. Add "They always did." only if there is room; the dry note is the campaign's and it is better on the instruction poster.

## Concept B, "Spread out" (blue), and B2 (lowercase, the post office line)

**Landlord.** This is the one they'd be pleased to be seen with. It isn't a telling-off; it is a cheerful instruction with the mark big. "Please don’t queue. Cheers." at the foot is exactly the tone a pub uses on its own signs ("Please don't feed the dog. Cheers."). Nothing on it is the pub's own line, though: a landlord who wants to say "this is how we do it here" has to read it as the campaign's poster that they happen to agree with. That is fine for the taproom and the craft bar; the traditional pub wants A or D.

**Drinker.** From the door: "SPREAD OUT." then the roundel. The instruction first, the picture of it second. That is the best reading order in the round, and it is the one the flyer couldn't manage because the stamp had to lead there. The subhead is the second read at the bar. "The bar is long for a reason." is a decent dry line; "It’s a pub. Not a post office." (B2) is our best line, and here, as the second read under the instruction, it finally has the context it lacked as the flyer's fourth read. Put it back. On case: the uppercase looks like a sign; the lowercase looks like the website. For a wall, sign.

**Calls to action.** "Sign the petition." and the code at the right, the polite line and the address at the left. One scan reason. The cta wraps in the render; the designer has the fix.

**Colour.** Blue is the reference's colour and it is handsome, but it means nothing in our system, and the drinker who has seen the site knows green is the Cluster. The "do" poster should be green (E's field). Then the pair is red/ink for "no" and green for "yes", and the two posters hung together are the split (C) done properly.

## Concept C, "Don't / do"

**Landlord.** It explains the term in one look (that, crossed out; this, instead), which is C's one real advantage: a stranger learns what "The Cluster" means without reading. But a landlord picks a sign in one voice, and this is two signs stapled together with the house line as a caption on the paper underneath.

**Drinker.** Two rings compete; the tenth shows neither leading. The house line, the cta and the address on white under the field look like the small print.

**Verdict.** Drop it, and get its benefit by offering the notice and the instruction poster as a pair on the download page ("Hang them together.").

## Concept D, "No queueing: the no-smoking structure" (ink, amber, red)

**Landlord.** This is the one. It is the point-of-sale notice every managed pub already has a drawer of, with the stock code and all, and the joke is that it looks official. "NO QUEUEING AT THE BAR" is in the register of the wall; "This bar does The Cluster." is the house line; "We kindly ask…" is the manners; the roundel and the address sign it as borrowed. A landlord can put this up without feeling they are joining a campaign, and by putting it up they have.

**Drinker.** From the door: amber headline, red ring. "NO QUEUEING" reads from further than anything but B's headline, and the red prohibition ring is the most recognised symbol on any wall. At the bar: the house line, then the ask. Reading order is textbook.

**Calls to action.** As A. Keep the stock code ("DQ/POS/BAR-001"): it is the funniest thing on any of the six and it is for the staff, who are the people who will decide whether the sign stays up.

**The catch.** It is a black page. A pub printing it at home will get a grey, banded, cartridge-draining sheet. So this concept only works if the mono version is as good as the colour one, and the download page offers the mono first for office printers. The colour version is the one to send to the print shop, or to laminate.

## Concept E, "Find the gap" (green)

**Landlord.** Green and cheerful, with the Tube joke, which lands with anyone who has lived in London and reads as a cheerful instruction to everyone else. "Any gap. The bar staff know who’s next." is step 2 and the reassurance; good. Same as B: the campaign's voice, not the house's.

**Drinker.** "FIND THE GAP." is a better instruction than "SPREAD OUT." for someone standing in a line: it tells them where to go, not just what to stop doing. The three-line stack is bigger at a tenth than anything but B, and the roundel is small by comparison.

**Calls to action.** "Take the test." is the right scan for this one: the drinker who has just been told to find a gap is the one who wants to know if they'd pass. But a poster on a wall wants the safer, shorter code (the home page's), and the home page links to the test. One sheet, one scan reason; if the family has two sheets, the notice sends people to the petition and the instruction poster to the test.

**Verdict.** The headline is a copy alternate for the instruction poster, not a third poster. Offer "SPREAD OUT." as the default and "FIND THE GAP." as the second line in the same layout if the team wants two.

## The pick

**Two posters, a pair: the notice (D's structure) for "don't", the instruction poster (B's structure, on green) for "do". Mono versions of both for the office printer. Uppercase headlines on both; the stamp keeps the lowercase.**

The notice is the landlord's sheet and the one most pubs will print; its mono has to be good. The instruction poster is the taproom's sheet and the campaign's happiest page. Together they teach the grammar (column, no; row, yes) that the split tried to teach on one sheet.

Decide the notice's field in Round 2 on two things: which reads as "the pub's notice" rather than "the campaign's poster" (ink, I think: red is a telling-off, ink is a rule), and which mono is better.
