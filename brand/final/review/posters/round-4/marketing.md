# Posters (notice family), round 4: marketing notes

Reviewed 14 Oct 2026 against the A4 renders and the tenths in this folder, with `../round-2/marketing.md` and the user's two asks. Judged on message, hierarchy and behaviour, not letterforms.

## The new polite line

"Please don’t single-file queue. Cheers." is better than "Please don’t queue. Cheers." for one reason that matters: it names the behaviour, not the customer. "Don’t queue" tells a person waiting for a drink they are doing something wrong; "don’t single-file queue" tells them which kind of waiting is wrong, and leaves the other kind (standing at the bar, which is also a queue, just an invisible one) alone. That is the campaign's whole argument in a polite line. It costs a second line at the foot and the roundel loses 6 mm; worth it. "Cheers." on its own line is fine; it reads as the sign-off it is.

## The along/away line

"Queue along the bar, not away from it" is the instruction the campaign has been circling for three rounds. "Spread out." says what to do; "find the gap" says where; this says both and names the mistake in the same breath. It is also the first line that explains the campaign's name: you may queue, just not away from the bar. For a drinker fourth in the line, "away from it" is a description of where they are standing. That is the line's power and its risk: it points at the person. The stress on *along* and *away* softens it, because it makes the sentence about direction rather than about them.

**Which sheet carries it.**

- **The headline poster (`along-amber`)** carries it best. From the door you read ALONG and AWAY in amber, then the white words, and the sentence assembles itself. It is the instruction poster in the campaign's voice with the user's line as the shout. On ink it sits beside the notice as a pair in the same colours, which a pub that prints both will like.
- **The notice's ask (`along-notice`)** should take the line. "We kindly ask that you queue along the bar, not away from it." is the pub's manners saying the whole instruction, where "spread out" said half of it. The render has a fault the designer has explained; the copy is right.
- **The two pictograms (`along-pictos`)** is the sheet for a pub whose customers need the picture: the row and the column, ticked and crossed, with the words under them. It is the explainer of the family and the best use of the prohibition sign we have. Keep the amber to the tick and ALONG: on this sheet amber should mean "this one", not "stressed".
- **The fragment (`along-fragment`)** is a good second headline for the taproom ("ALONG. NOT AWAY." is a chant) but it needs the subhead to mean anything, so it doesn't replace the sentence.
- **Weight and underline** are not copy questions; the designer's call stands.
- **Lowercase** is the website's voice; these are the pub's. Uppercase stays.

**The copy, final.** Headline: "ALONG THE BAR. NOT AWAY FROM IT." Ask: "We kindly ask that you queue along the bar, not away from it. The bar staff know who’s next." Subhead and pictogram labels: "Queue along the bar, not away from it." and "ALONG" / "AWAY". The user's full phrasing, with "the bar" twice, is the spoken form and stays in the brief as the source; the sheets use "not away from it" because the second "the bar" is the fourth read on a wall.

## Which to ship

Five sheets, and the download page groups them in two rows:

- **Notices** (the landlord's row, mono first): "NO QUEUEING AT THE BAR" with the new ask; "QUEUE ALONG THE BAR." with the two pictograms.
- **Posters** (the taproom's row): "SPREAD OUT." on green; "ALONG THE BAR. NOT AWAY FROM IT." on ink; "FIND THE GAP." as the alternate if two green posters are wanted.

One line under both rows: colour sheets for a colour printer or a print shop; the black-and-white ones for the office laser; hang a notice and a poster together.
