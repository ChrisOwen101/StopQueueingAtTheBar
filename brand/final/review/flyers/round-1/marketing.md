# Flyers, round 1: marketing notes

Reviewed 13 Oct 2026 against the A4 renders in this folder and the tenth-size thumbnails. Judged on message, hierarchy and behaviour, not letterforms (the renders are Inter; the final is Avenir Next).

## The frame first

Two people have to say yes to this sheet, and they want different things.

- **The landlord** prints it because the single file annoys them: it blocks the room, makes a four-person bar look like one till, and they can't say "spread out" to a customer without sounding rude. The poster's job for them is to say it in someone else's voice. The campaign takes the blame; the pub borrows the line. Nobody prints a poster for a stranger's petition. They print a sign they wanted anyway.
- **The drinker** standing fourth in the line reads it from the wall. What stops them leaving the queue isn't ignorance, it's the fear of looking like they're pushing in. So the poster has to do three things in order: give permission (the pub says so), say what to do instead (spread out along the bar), and reassure (the bar staff know who's next). The joke is there so none of that feels like a telling off.

That gives the hierarchy of asks for the whole set:

1. **Spread out.** The behaviour, here, tonight. Primary on the poster.
2. **Scan.** The route to the site. Secondary, at the foot, with a reason next to it.
3. **Sign the petition.** The campaign's measure. The reason to scan on Face A.
4. **Take the test.** The fun route in. The reason to scan on Face B, where the reader has just learned the steps and "think you're ready?" is the natural next line.

Two things to keep off a pub wall: any figure that could be read as the pub's own waiting time (so the worked example stays on Face B, in the hand, never on the poster), and anything in the pub's voice that teases their customers (the jokes stay signed by the campaign, not the house).

## Decision: the pub-facing line goes on the poster

Yes. "This bar does The Cluster." belongs on Face A, and it is the single biggest reason a landlord would print this rather than nod at it.

Reasons:

- It turns a campaign poster into a house sign, in the register pubs already use ("this bar is cashless", "table service only", "no, we don't do halves of Guinness"). That is a thing a landlord puts up. A campaign poster is a thing they're asked to put up.
- It supplies the permission the drinker needs. "Says who?" is answered: this bar.
- It is positive. It says what the house does, not what the customer is doing wrong. "Don't queue at the bar" on its own (Concept 1) is a prohibition with no replacement, and a person already standing in the line reads it as being told off.
- It is the first pub pledge. A pub that prints it has joined. That's the "pub-pledge form for venues" from the open items, done with a printer.

The risk is a pub that wants the poster but not the commitment. I'd accept that. The line is the point; a pub that won't say "this bar does The Cluster" won't enforce it either, and the poster without it is just the site's homepage on paper. If the team wants a fallback, the stamp-plus-"spread out." version (Face A with the house line removed) is a one-line change, but I wouldn't offer it on the download page. One poster.

"It's a pub. Not a post office." stays, but as the strap, not the headline. It's the campaign's best joke and it explains the name, but a joke alone doesn't move anyone out of a queue, and "post office" read from across the room without context is a puzzle. So the poster carries both: the house line gives permission, "spread out." gives the instruction, the strap gives the laugh.

## Concept 1, "Stamp first"

**Landlord.** It looks like a brand poster, which is to say it looks like someone else's advert. Nothing on it is the pub's. The only instruction is the name, and the name is a prohibition: a landlord reads it as "a sign telling my customers off, with my pub's name nowhere near it to soften it". It doesn't shame the pub, but it gives the landlord nothing to gain by putting it up beyond being a good sport.

**Drinker.** Reading order is clean: stamp, tagline, foot. From across the room (the tenth) the whole message is "don't queue at the bar." The tagline is just legible; the foot isn't. The message is clear but negative: what shouldn't I do, and then what? There is no "spread out" anywhere on the face.

**Calls to action.** "Sign the petition." is the campaign's ask placed as the poster's only ask. On a wall, the behaviour should come first. The route is split across the foot: the caption and address bottom left, the QR bottom right, with a roundel bottom left as well. Three marks on one sheet (stamp, roundel, roundel in the QR). One mark per job: the stamp leads, the roundel sits in the QR, and the foot needs nothing else.

**Face B.** The best Face B of the three. Kicker, headline, a vertical list of the four steps with the site's lines verbatim, a heavy rule, then the maths. Reads top to bottom in the hand with nothing fighting. Problems: a third of the page is blank between the maths and the foot, which looks unfinished rather than calm, and the "worked example" stamp is doing the caveat on its own (see below).

**Copy, line by line.**

- Stamp: keep. It leads, as the brief says it should.
- "It's a pub. Not a post office.": right joke, wrong job on its own. Demote to the strap.
- Missing: "spread out." Add it as the headline.
- "Sign the petition." plus address: keep the words, move them next to the QR.
- Face B kicker "how to do the cluster" and headline "four easy steps.": keep (the site's own).
- Steps 1 to 4: keep verbatim.
- "25 minutes. or six.": keep in headline style (lowercase big type, as the other heads).
- Supporting line "Twenty orders, 75 seconds each. One spot on the bar, or four staff along it.": keep, and add the caveat (below).

## Concept 2, "House rule"

**Landlord.** This is the one they'd print. "this bar does the cluster." is their line; the stamp top left signs it as borrowed, so the humour is the campaign's and the house is just adopting the rule. It doesn't tell customers off, and it doesn't shame the pub. Two things would make a landlord hesitate: "house rules" as a kicker top right reads like a pool-table notice, and Face B's "the rules of this bar." puts the jokes ("this is not a cuddle", "it's their entire job") and the 25-minute figure in the pub's own mouth. A pub doesn't want "rules" on its counter and it certainly doesn't want to look like it's promising six-minute service.

**Drinker.** Headline first, then the subhead, then the small stamp, then the foot. From across the room "this bar does the cluster." reads clearly and is a hook ("the what?"), but the actual instruction, spread out, is body text and doesn't survive the tenth. The hook has no payoff at distance.

**Calls to action.** "The whole story, and a petition." is apologetic. "And a petition" sounds like something you'd rather not mention. Say "Sign the petition." and mean it.

**Brand.** The stamp as a letterhead is below "the stamp leads on flyers" in the brief. It can be smaller than Concept 1's, but it has to be the first mark you see, not a logo in the corner.

**Face B.** The ruled lines between steps are the best detail in the round for in-hand reading; keep them. The boxed worked example separates the sum from the steps; keep that too. Drop the "rules of this bar" framing.

**Copy, line by line.**

- "house rules" (kicker, top right): drop. The headline says it.
- "this bar does the cluster.": keep, as the permission line.
- "Spread out along the bar. The bar staff know who's next. They always did.": split. "spread out." goes big; the rest becomes the body: "Along the bar, not behind each other. The bar staff know who's next. They always did."
- "The whole story, and a petition.": change to "Sign the petition."
- Face B "house rules" / "the rules of this bar.": change to the site's kicker and headline, with the house line folded into the kicker: "this bar does the cluster. here's how." / "four easy steps."
- Steps 1. to 4.: keep verbatim; the trailing full stops after the numerals are fine with the rules, or drop them to match Concept 1. Either, but one style across both faces.

## Concept 3, "The bar"

**Landlord.** "spread out." is an order, but a cheerful one, and "it's a pub. not a post office." teases the queue, not the customer or the pub, so it doesn't shame anyone. But there's no "here": nothing says the pub agrees. It's a campaign poster again, with a better instruction than Concept 1.

**Drinker.** From across the room, "spread out." reads first (it's the biggest), then the stamp, then "it's a pub. not a post office." That is the right order of ideas, but three elements of similar weight compete, the headline crowds the stamp's clear space, and the black band doesn't read as a bar. It reads as a rule. The idea of the bar as a motif needs the roundel's overhanging counter to say "bar", and the brief says don't redraw the mark, so the band is a divider, not a drawing. Drop it.

**Calls to action.** Same foot as Concept 1, same split. "Sign the petition." plus address, with the QR on the other side.

**Face B.** The four columns don't work. Column 3 is twice the depth of column 2, so the eye drops into the ragged bottom edge; at A5 the columns would be too narrow to set at all. "four easy steps." over two lines plus the band spends half the page before step 1. Use the list.

**Copy, line by line.**

- "it's a pub. not a post office." (headline): demote to the strap.
- "spread out.": keep, big.
- "Along the bar, not behind each other. The barperson knows who's next. They always did.": keep. On the poster, "bar staff" (plural) over "barperson", because the point of the poster is four staff along one bar, and in the house voice the pub is talking about its own staff. Keep "barperson" on Face B step 4, which is the site's line.
- Face B: the steps verbatim; the layout is the problem, not the words.

## Is "It's a pub. Not a post office." the right poster line?

No, not on its own. It's the right strap. On its own it's a joke with no instruction (Concept 1) or a second headline fighting the first (Concept 3). The poster carries both: the house line leads for the pub, "spread out." leads for the room, and the post office line signs it off in the campaign's voice. If the design is crowded, the strap is the first thing to cut, because the stamp already carries the name's joke.

## The route to the site

QR plus a written address is enough for a pub. Since 2020 everyone in a pub has scanned a menu; the irony is free. The written address is only a backstop for the photo-of-the-poster case, so it should be one unbroken line, lowercase, no "https://", and small.

The line next to the QR should say what you get, not "scan me":

- Face A: **Sign the petition.** The campaign's measure, one tap from the home page.
- Face B: **Think you're ready? Take the test.** The reader has just learned the four steps; the site's own kicker does the join, and the test is the better lure for someone with a pint in hand and two minutes.

Both QR codes go to the home page, as the mat back does, so the codes stay valid whatever the site does with the test and petition pages. Put the caption beside the QR, not across the page from it.

On the address itself: the workers.dev address looks temporary on paper, and if the Worker ever moves, every printed sheet dies. The mats already assume dontqueueatthebar.co.uk. Buy the domain before the download goes live. Never print a URL you plan to change. If it has to ship before then, the long address goes on, small, and the QR carries the weight. The roundel tile in the QR (Concept 1's) should stay for consistency with the mat back, at the size the mat tests passed, and nothing larger.

## The worked example on Face B

Yes, it earns its place, as the "why" after the "how". A drinker who has just read four steps needs one reason to bother, and "25 minutes. or six." is the only real-ish number the campaign allows itself. In the hand, next to the steps, it's fine.

The label on its own isn't clear enough. "worked example" is a stamp that means "this is a sum, not a stopwatch" to anyone who did GCSE maths and a decorative badge to everyone else. The supporting line already reads as a sum ("Twenty orders, 75 seconds each. One spot on the bar, or four staff along it."), which helps, but a sheet left on a bar can still be read as "this pub makes you wait 25 minutes". Kill that reading in the copy: add "Arithmetic, not a stopwatch. Real pubs are messier." The second sentence is the site's own line. Keep the stamp, keep the line.

Never put the figure on Face A. On a wall it becomes the pub's waiting time.

YouGov's 40/39 split: leave it off both faces. It's a good line on the site ("one point in it") but on a poster the first number says "most people prefer the queue", which is the wrong thing to print on a pub wall, and the flyers should carry one idea each.

## The pick

**Merge Concept 2 and Concept 3 for Face A. Use Concept 1's Face B with Concept 2's ruled lines.**

Face A takes the house line from Concept 2 (the reason a landlord prints it), "spread out." from Concept 3 (the across-the-room instruction), the post office line as the strap, and the stamp at a size that leads, as the brief requires. Reading order from the door: stamp, "spread out.". Reading order at the bar: "This bar does The Cluster.", the body, the strap, the QR. One idea, said three ways, stepping down in size.

Face B stays in the campaign's voice (kicker, headline, list, rule, maths, foot), because that's the only voice in which the jokes and the sum are safe, with the house line folded into the kicker so a sheet printed alone still carries the pub's permission. The worked example gets its caveat. The scan reason is the test.

Drop: Concept 1's bare prohibition, Concept 2's "rules of this bar", Concept 3's columns and black band, all three foot layouts that split the QR from its caption, and the second roundel at the foot.

## Final copy

### Face A: the poster

Read from across the room. Sizes step down in the order listed.

```
[the stamp: don’t queue / at the bar.]        leads; clear space kept

spread out.                                   headline, the biggest words

This bar does The Cluster.                    second, the pub’s line

Along the bar, not behind each other.         body
The bar staff know who’s next.
They always did.

It’s a pub. Not a post office.                strap, the campaign’s sign-off

[QR, roundel tile]  Sign the petition.        foot, caption beside the code
                    dont-queue-at-the-bar.aged-mouse-6395.workers.dev
```

If the sheet is crowded: cut the strap before anything else. Never cut the house line or "spread out."

### Face B: the how-to

Read in the hand. Vertical list, a rule between steps, the sum below a heavy rule or in a box.

```
this bar does the cluster. here’s how.        kicker

four easy steps.                              headline

1  walk to the bar.
   Not behind someone. To the bar. It’s the long wooden thing.

2  pick a gap.
   Any gap. Leave some elbow room. This is not a cuddle.

3  catch an eye.
   A nod. A raised eyebrow. A tenner held with quiet confidence.
   No waving. No clicking.

4  trust the barperson.
   They know who’s next. They always know. It’s their entire job.

25 minutes. or six.                           [worked example]
Twenty orders, 75 seconds each. One spot on the bar,
or four staff along it. Arithmetic, not a stopwatch.
Real pubs are messier.

[QR, roundel tile]  Think you’re ready? Take the test.
                    dont-queue-at-the-bar.aged-mouse-6395.workers.dev

[the stamp, small]                            bottom right, the signature
```

If Face B must also carry the petition because it's printed alone: "Take the test. Then sign the petition." as one bold line. I'd rather each face earned one scan.

### Notes for the download page, not the sheet

- "Stick it behind the bar, facing the room." The poster works where the back of the line can see it.
- Both faces fit A5 if the body stays to three lines; an office printer does A4, so offer the A4 PDF and let them scale.
- Typographic apostrophes throughout ("don’t", "It’s", "who’s"), as the brief's open item says.
- Gender check passed on the copy above: barperson, bar staff, they/them only; no gendered job titles or pronouns.
