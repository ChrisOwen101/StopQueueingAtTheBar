# Homepage, round 1: the copy deck

Written 14 Oct 2026 by the copywriter, against `BRIEF.md`, the live `public/index.html`, the poll and form words in `public/script.js` and `src/worker.js`, `RESEARCH.md`, and the flyer decisions in `brand/final/review/flyers/round-1/`. Organised by the job the words do, not by the current screens, because the designer will reorder screens.

How to read it:

- Three options for every headline, the pick marked **pick** with a line on why. Supporting lines get one or two options. Where the current line is right I say **keep** and move on.
- Headlines are written in sentence case; the CSS sets `.mega`, `.big` and `.rule-title` lowercase, so "The Cluster" in a headline renders "the cluster". That is style, not spelling. Where a capital carries meaning it is in body text, tags, kickers' neighbours and buttons, which the CSS leaves alone: **The Cluster**, **The Single File**, and the name, which keeps its capitals on the hero (the one `.mega` that isn't lowercased; keep that).
- Every figure traces to a row in `RESEARCH.md`. Where a new line needs a row ticked or a link added before it ships, I say so next to it.
- Voice check passed on every line below: barperson, bar staff, landlord, they/them; typographic apostrophes; no exclamation marks, no "Let’s", no "It’s not X, it’s Y", no "imagine", no "we believe", no three-adjective lists. One exception, argued for in section 1.

## 1. The hero

The name stays: **Don’t Queue At The Bar.**

### The strap

1. **It’s a pub. Not a post office.** **Pick. Keep.**
   Marketing cut it from the poster because it was the fourth thing read, and nobody reads the fourth thing on a poster. On the hero it is the second thing read, directly under the name, which is the job it was written for: it explains the name’s joke and sets the voice in seven words. The share image carries it, so the card people see in WhatsApp and the page they land on say the same thing. LBC used the phrase on air, so the public already has it.
   On the `TODO.md` rule: this is the positive cousin of "It’s not X, it’s Y". It stays because it was here first and the rule was written about its copies. Nothing else in this deck uses the shape, in either direction. One per site.
2. Spread out. The bar staff know who’s next.
   The poster’s instruction and reassurance, so print and site open with the same words. Loses the laugh, and the hero is where the laugh earns the scroll.
3. Stand at the bar. Any bit of it.
   The instruction with the joke folded in. Second choice if the team wants an instruction above the fold.

### The five-word line (what the site wants from you)

Under the strap, `.mid` or `.small`, a plain link. The ask here is the vote, because it costs nothing, takes one tap, and the petition follows it (section 6). Never both in the hero.

1. **Pick a side. One tap.** **Pick.** It reuses the poll’s own kicker, promises something that takes a second, and if the vote pill is built the link points at the pill and the visitor never scrolls for it. Link: the pill, else `#poll`.
2. Had enough? Vote here.
3. A vote, then a pint.

All three fit on one line at 390 wide.

If the designer prefers the petition count in the hero instead, use the count line in section 6 and drop this one. The hero carries one ask, not two.

### The promo sticker

Keep, with the apostrophe: **Get your pub-goer’s licence today.** It is a possessive, and "pub goers licence" at that size reads as a typo. The small line **Do the hazard perception test →** stays.

### The money, three ways (each tagged **worked example** or **illustrative**)

The frame: nothing here measures a pub. Every number is the register’s row M arithmetic (75 seconds an order, four staff) and the register’s one price (the average draught pint, £5.34, Morning Advertiser, Apr 2026). The line never says "losing"; it says "waiting", because that is what a sum can honestly say.

**(a) Throughput.** Heading **48 an hour. Or 192.** Line: **One spot on the bar gets through 48 orders an hour. Four staff along it, 192. Same staff, same bar, no line.** Tag: worked example. (3,600 ÷ 75 = 48; × 4 = 192.)

**(b) Money, pick for the page.** **On the worked example’s Friday, 90 orders an hour, one spot on the bar serves 48. That leaves 42 an hour in a line. At the average pint, £5.34, that’s about £224 an hour waiting to be poured.** Caveat under it: **Illustrative. The pint is the UK average (Morning Advertiser, Apr 2026). The Friday is made up. Your pub is yours.** (42 × £5.34 = £224.28. This is the Friday line from section 4, moved here and priced.)
Alternative, capacity not waiting: **At £5.34 a pint, one spot on the bar can ring up about £256 an hour. Four along it, about £1,025. Capacity, not takings.** (48 × £5.34 = £256.32; 192 × £5.34 = £1,025.28.) Bigger numbers, and they read like a promise; I’d use (b) and keep this for a press line with the caveat attached.

**(c) The calculator.** Heading **Do the sum for your pub.**
- Input: **Bar staff on tonight** (stepper, 1 to 8, default 4).
- Input: **Your pint** (£, default 5.34, hint **UK average draught pint, Apr 2026**).
- Output, two lines: **In a line, one of you serves: 48 orders an hour, about £256.** / **Spread out, all 4 of you serve: 192 an hour, about £1,025.**
- Caveat: **A sum, not a forecast: 75 seconds an order, a pint an order, and only if the orders are there. Nobody has timed your pub.**
- With one member of staff the two lines come out the same, so the output says: **With one of you, the line and The Cluster come out the same. The Cluster still takes less room.** No number.

Never on this screen or anywhere: "you’re losing £X", "pubs lose", a town’s pint price, a named pub. The feedback’s "in Neath the pints are this much" is a PR route for marketing, and it needs a local price in the register before it is a sentence.

## The three decisions I feel strongest about

1. **The headlines state the idea and the names move to the kickers.** "Four staff. One working." over the problem, "Spread out." over the solution, "the single file" and "the cluster" as the kickers that teach the terms. The feedback said the headline over the comparison described the staging; these say the thing, and the second one is the poster’s word, so the site, the wall and the mat finally say the same thing in the same place.
